Early ice crappies

The easiest panfish fishing of the year happens in the first two weeks of safe ice on the Eagle River Chain, and hardly anyone is out there for it.

JournalArea GuideWhere to EatFishingThe Cabins

The first couple of weeks of safe ice are the easiest panfish fishing of the whole year, and almost nobody is out there for it. The crappies have not been bothered since the docks came out, they are still schooled tight from late fall, and a bucket and a short rod are genuinely all you need. Here is how we fish early ice for crappies and bluegills on the Eagle River Chain and the Sugar Camp Chain.

Timing

Why is early ice the best panfish window?

Two things line up at once. The fish have had weeks of quiet since the boats came off the water, and the ice is still clear and snow-free, so you can walk a lot of water quickly and let the lake tell you where they are instead of committing to one hole and hoping. Crappies school tight in late fall and stay schooled through freeze-up, which means the difference between a slow afternoon and a full bucket is often fifty yards.

The window does not last. Once snow loads the ice, light drops at the bottom, the schools spread out, and the easy bite settles into the grind most people picture when they think of ice fishing. Before you step out on any of it, though, read our notes on reading first ice — on a chain of lakes that part is not optional, and no bite is worth guessing about.

Location

Where do the schools sit on a chain like ours?

Early ice is a soft-bottom game. The spots to start are the same ones that held fish in late fall: the deepest hole in a shallow back bay, the basin just outside a weedbed that is still green, and the first drop where a flat turns to mud. Crappies suspend — often well up off the bottom — while bluegills sit tighter to it, usually in or against whatever weed is left standing.

A chain adds one wrinkle worth knowing. The channels between the lakes carry current all winter, and that current both concentrates fish along the edge where moving water meets still water and keeps the ice there thin long after the lake looks locked up. Those spots are worth fishing and worth respecting.

Back-bay holes

Six to twelve feet of soft bottom in a sheltered bay. First to freeze, first to fish, and often the best crappie water on the chain in December.

Weed edges

The outside edge of green weed on a flat. Bluegills live in it, crappies cruise just off it. Fish the edge rather than the middle.

Basin flats

Mud bottom in the mid-teens to low twenties. Where the schools slide once the shallow bays have seen some traffic. Look up, not down.

Channel edges

Where current meets still water. Fish stack here and so does thin ice, so treat them as places to approach carefully, never as a shortcut across.

Gear

What do you actually need to fish it?

Less than the trucks parked on the ice would suggest. A light rod, a couple of spools of light line, a handful of small tungsten jigs, waxies or soft plastics, a spud bar, an auger and a five-gallon bucket will catch panfish all day. A flasher or an underwater camera makes it easier to find fish and much easier to stay patient, but plenty of people up here still fish by feel and do fine.

Two habits matter more than any of that. Drill more holes than you think you need and move on after ten quiet minutes — at first ice the fish are findable, so hunting beats waiting. And start your bait above the fish, because a crappie will rise to look at something and almost never drops down for it. If you would rather set lines and watch them, our comparison of tip-ups and jigging covers when each approach earns its place.

How we do it

What does an early-ice afternoon look like?

Out mid-morning, a line of holes drilled across a bay from shallow to deep, ten minutes in each one, and the fish decide the depth rather than us. When a school turns up we stay quiet, keep the hole clear and fish small. By late afternoon the bite usually tightens up for a while right before dark, which is the part worth staying out for. Keep your regulations current before you keep anything — panfish bag limits and lake-specific rules change, so check the Wisconsin DNR fishing regulations for the water you are on.

Good to know

Common questions

Where do crappies go at first ice on the Eagle River Chain?
Look for soft bottom in sheltered water first. The deepest hole in a shallow back bay, the outside edge of weed that is still green, and the first drop onto a mud flat all hold early-ice crappies on this chain. Crappies suspend well off the bottom, so find the depth before you settle in, and remember that the schools slide deeper as the bays get more pressure through December.
What depth should you start at for early-ice panfish?
Start shallow and work out. Six to twelve feet of soft bottom in a protected bay is the classic first-ice depth for crappies and bluegills up here, and the mid-teens to low twenties take over once the fish have been bothered for a few weeks. Drill a line of holes from shallow to deep rather than guessing, and fish each one for about ten minutes before you move.
Do you need a shelter to fish early ice for panfish?
No. A bucket, a short rod, a spud bar and an auger will catch fish all day at first ice, and travelling light is an advantage when the whole point is covering water. A shelter earns its keep later in the winter, when the wind picks up, the snow arrives and you are settling into one spot for hours instead of moving every ten minutes.
